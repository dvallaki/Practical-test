One task, six steps, two hours.

Rules

  • AI tools are allowed and expected. Claude Code, Cursor, Copilot, whatever you normally use. We build this way every day. What we grade is what sits on top of the tool: whether you read what came back, whether you notice when it is subtly wrong, and whether you can defend every line. 90% AI-generated and fully understood beats hand-typed. Anything you cannot explain scores zero.
  • Two hours, and it is a real cap. The repo already runs, so none of it goes on setup. Say in the README where the time went and what you did not reach. Nobody has been marked down for "I ran out of time before X". Eight hours against a two hour brief is a worse answer, not a better one.
  • Send it back within 3 calendar days, then a 30 minute screen share where you walk us through it and make a small change live, with your AI tools open. Build with that in mind.
  • The spec below came from the client, left exactly as it arrived. Client specs are rarely perfect. Spotting what is unclear, missing or contradictory and raising it is part of this job. We will not tell you whether there is anything wrong with this one. Whatever you find goes in the README.
  • Email us if something blocks you. Asking a good question is not a mark against you here, closer to the opposite.

What you are building

The client runs feedback surveys for three of their own enterprise customers. Reporting today is a monthly spreadsheet stitched together by hand, and they want a small web app instead. You are building one vertical slice: get their export into a proper relational shape, and put one report on the screen.

Start here

docker compose up --build
  • App: http://localhost:5173
  • API health: http://localhost:8000/api/health
  • Run the ingest: docker compose exec backend python -m app.ingest
  • Run the tests: docker compose exec backend pytest

Done for you: Postgres, FastAPI with hot reload, the Vite React app, the fetch layer, and the survey list with its loading and error states.

Yours: models.py (empty), normalize.py (three signatures), ingest.py (stub), two endpoint stubs in main.py, one TODO in frontend/src/App.tsx, and task-b/.

The data, read-only at /data: surveys.csv, 12 surveys with their invitation counts, and responses.csv, about 38,000 rows. That export came out of the client's legacy tool and has been through three system migrations. Assume nothing about it.

Swap a library if you strongly prefer another, say why in the README, and know that you are spending your own two hours on it.

The task

Six steps. Times are a guide and roughly how we weight it.

Step 1. Data model - 20 min

Design and create the schema for surveys and responses in backend/app/models.py. This is the heaviest item on the scoresheet, so spend real thought here. There is no fixed number of tables we want.

Build the tables however you like: create_all(), Alembic, a plain .sql file. We grade the schema, not the migration tool.

Done when: the tables exist, and the README explains your keys, your constraints, your indexes and anything you made nullable on purpose.

Step 2. Ingest - 30 min

Fill in backend/app/ingest.py so it loads both CSVs into your schema, and use the three helpers from Step 3 rather than doing the same work inline.

The client's rules for the import:

  1. A bad row must not stop the import. Bad rows go to rejects.csv with the reason they were rejected.
  2. Ratings are 1 to 5. Anything outside that range is a data error. Reject it and log it. Do not clamp it into range.
  3. Dates arrive day first, DD/MM/YYYY HH:MM:SS, with no timezone marker. All of it is Asia/Kolkata local time. A second customer is being onboarded in Dubai next quarter, so store timestamps in a way that will not have to be re-migrated when that happens.
  4. A respondent counts only once per survey. Their email addresses arrive from three different client systems and have never been normalised.
  5. Re-running the ingest on the same file must not double the data.

Done when: the ingest runs to completion, rejects.csv carries a real reason per row, running it twice changes nothing, and the README states rows loaded, rows rejected, and the distinct reasons.

Step 3. normalize.py - 15 min, graded on its own

backend/app/normalize.py holds three signatures with empty bodies:

parse_client_datetime(raw)   -> datetime | None
normalise_email(raw)         -> str | None
parse_duration_seconds(raw)  -> int | None

The docstrings in that file are the contract. Read them. After you submit we drop our own pytest file into backend/tests/ and run it against this module unchanged, so keep the signatures exactly as they are and keep the functions pure. The six examples in test_normalize_examples.py show the shape only. They are not the tests we grade with, and passing them proves very little.

Done when: your ingest imports these three functions and uses them, and the signatures are untouched.

Step 4. The report endpoint - 20 min

GET /api/surveys/{survey_id}/summary, broken down by reporting week, returning week_start, responses_started, responses_completed, completion_rate, median_duration_seconds.

The client's rules, verbatim from their email:

  1. "Our reporting week runs Saturday to Friday. It always has. Every other report we get uses this and the numbers have to tie out."
  2. "Completion rate is completed responses over invitations sent for that survey. Not over the responses we received. The whole point is to see who never turned up."
  3. "A partial response counts in the response count. It does not count as completed. abandoned and started count in neither."
  4. "It has to come back fast enough to click through surveys without waiting. Under 300 milliseconds."

GET /api/surveys is also stubbed and the front end already calls it. Return whatever the list page needs.

Done when: you have read your own output back against all four rules line by line, point 4 holds against the fully loaded dataset, and the README says how you measured it and what number you got.

Step 5. The page - 15 min

Finish the one page. The list and its states are already there; the SurveySummary component in App.tsx is a TODO. Selecting a survey shows its weekly summary table.

We are not grading visual design. Plain, readable and correct beats styled and wrong. Two things are graded, because they are not decoration:

  • Loading and error states on the summary, the same way the list already does it.
  • The page has to survive every survey in the list, not just the ones with data.

Done when: you have clicked through all twelve surveys yourself and none of them shows a crash, a blank, or a nonsense number.

Step 6. Two write-ups - 20 min

6a. The delete. Design only, no code.

The client wants to delete a survey from the list page. They told us two things, in two separate emails.

Their head of compliance, 4 August:

"When we delete a survey, every response attached to it has to be gone permanently. That is a compliance requirement from our customer's legal team, and it is not negotiable."

Their reporting manager, 11 August:

"The audit table has to keep a record of every response ever submitted, including ones belonging to surveys that were later deleted. We reconcile historical counts against it at year end."

Do not build this. Write six to ten lines in the README: what you would do, and why.

6b. Review a pull request

This one is not about your code. It is a review of someone else's.

A junior developer on the team has opened a pull request. It is sitting in task-b/, three files:

File What it is
task-b/review_this.py a FastAPI router
task-b/review_this_test.py its tests
task-b/ReviewThis.tsx a React component

Their PR description, in full:

"Generated most of this with Cursor, tests pass, ready for review."

The tests do pass. You are the reviewer on this PR, and your review is the only thing between it and main.

All three files are in scope. Do not change them and do not fix what you find, we are reading the review, not a patch. Your own Steps 1 to 5 work is not part of this and belongs in the README. REVIEW.md covers task-b/ and nothing else.

Write REVIEW.md, and keep to this shape, because we read it for judgement rather than volume:

  • Line one: merge or block. If block, which findings are the blockers.
  • Your top five findings, ranked, worst first. Not your top twenty. Choosing what to leave out is most of the exercise.
  • Each finding: file and line, what actually breaks, what should happen instead. Two or three sentences.
  • One thing that is fine. Something that looks alarming but is acceptable in context, and why.

Padding costs you marks. A review that names the three things that matter beats one that lists everything at equal weight. Write it the way you would actually leave it for the author, who is junior and will read it.
